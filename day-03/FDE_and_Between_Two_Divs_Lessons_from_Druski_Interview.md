# FDE and Between Two Divs: lessons from the Druski interview

## My north star

I want to become a forward deployed AI engineer who can understand a customer's real problem, build and deploy a useful solution, train people to use it, and measure whether it works. Through **Between Two Divs**, I want to help Oklahoma schools, after school programs, and nonprofits—especially in rural towns—train staff and students and create real pathways into tech work. My current Douglass High School tech internship program is a starting point. The goal is to prepare seniors for internship opportunities next summer, then build a program that could eventually be among the strongest tech workforce development programs nationally.

**Working thesis:** My education experience gives me a useful FDE advantage in discovery, facilitation, training, and adoption. I must pair it with demonstrated engineering delivery: working software, integrations, evaluations, deployment, security, and support.

## Two connected tracks

### Track A — Become a credible FDE

1. **Discover:** Interview a real partner about the workflow, people affected, success criteria, constraints, and current workarounds before proposing AI.
2. **Build:** Ship a narrow tool or integration in Python/FastAPI with a real user flow; use an LLM only when it improves the outcome.
3. **Evaluate:** Test with realistic tasks; record quality, failure cases, human review needs, latency, and cost.
4. **Deploy and operate:** Host it, log useful events without exposing student information, handle errors, and make a handoff guide.
5. **Train and measure adoption:** Teach staff how to use it; observe whether the workflow improves after training.

My portfolio case study should show the **problem → discovery evidence → design decisions → working demo → evaluation → deployment → adoption/results**. A workshop alone demonstrates teaching; this complete sequence demonstrates forward deployed engineering.

### Track B — Build Between Two Divs into a repeatable workforce program

1. **Discover local needs:** Ask each school or nonprofit about student goals, staff capacity, transportation, devices, connectivity, employer relationships, and scheduling.
2. **Run the Douglass pilot:** Teach a defined set of skills, collect student work, support mentors, and prepare the summer internship pathway. Record actual placements only after they occur.
3. **Create reusable pieces:** A facilitator guide, student project rubrics, employer project briefs, mentor orientation, internship readiness rubric, and partner reporting template.
4. **Test in a rural setting:** Co-design with one willing Oklahoma school, after school program, or nonprofit. Adapt for local resources and travel rather than assuming the Douglass model will transfer unchanged.
5. **Prove repeatability:** Compare outcomes across sites, document what needed adaptation, and build a partner and employer network that can support each cohort.

Oklahoma CareerTech already operates a statewide technology center network and describes work-based learning as a connection between education and industry. Explore how to complement local partners rather than duplicate their services. Its work-based learning guidance outlines roles such as employer task design, intern preparation, supervisor check-ins, and evaluations. See official resources below.

| Days 61–90 | Hold a showcase; confirm employer interest and remaining gaps before next summer's placements. Write a case study and a repeatable partner kit. | Student opt-in showcase, mentor feedback, readiness assessment, employer commitments, cost and time per learner. |

**Outcome ladder:** Attendance and interest are early signals. Completed projects and skill gains are stronger evidence. Interviews, internships, successful completion, references, continued study, and paid work are progressively stronger outcomes. Report the number of eligible participants and the time window with every percentage. Avoid implying a training program caused an outcome without supporting evidence.

## Questions to take into a partner discovery meeting

- What would better tech training let your students or staff do six months from now?
- Where do students currently lose momentum between interest, skill building, and real work?
- What devices, internet access, transportation, staff time, and accessible supports are available?
- Which local employers or community organizations could supply age-appropriate projects and mentors?
- What existing CareerTech, district, ICAP, after school, or nonprofit programs should we coordinate with?
- What would make a pilot successful enough for you to run it again? What would make you stop?
- Who can approve participation, data use, student work publication, and internships? What supervision and reporting does the school require?

## Constraints and guardrails I want to keep

- **Protect stable income and program quality.** The interview's extreme risk and nonstop work are part of one person's story, not a business requirement. Build a sustainable FDE career and organization.
- **Do not confuse recognition with outcomes.** A big partner name, viral post, or event turnout does not prove students gained skills or entered internships.
- **Avoid a technology-first pitch.** Some partners need employer coordination, teacher training, or simple process changes more than an AI product.
- **Treat minors' information carefully.** Collect only what is needed; confirm school policies, consent, access, retention, and publication rules before using real student data in a portfolio or AI system.
- **Include the local community.** Design rural offerings with school staff, families, students, and employers. Make room for local strengths and constraints.
